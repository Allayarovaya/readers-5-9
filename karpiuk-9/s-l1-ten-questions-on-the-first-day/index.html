Lesson 1

Ten Questions on the First Day

Listen & Read
Listen to the text
Speed:
On the first of September our English teacher, Ms Olena, came into the classroom with a small box. "I am not going to ask you how you spent your summer," she said. "You are going to ask each other. But there is one rule: no boring questions." There were thirty cards in the box. Each of us took one and read the name of a classmate on it. I got Davyd. We have been in the same class for eight years, and I was sure that I knew everything about him. He likes football, hates Maths and always forgets his pen. What could I ask? "You have ten minutes to write ten questions," said Ms Olena. "Start each question with a different word: who, what, where, when, why, which, whose, how, how many, how often." It was harder than it looked. My first question was "Where you went in summer?" Ms Olena looked over my shoulder and smiled. "Where is the helping verb, Marta? Where did you go?" I corrected it and went on. Then we worked in pairs. I asked my questions, and Davyd's answers surprised me. "Where did you go in July?" "Nowhere. I stayed at home and worked." "What did you do?" "I helped my uncle to repair bikes in his workshop." "How many bikes did you repair?" "Forty-three. I counted." "Why did you do it?" "I wanted to earn money for a telescope." "Whose idea was the telescope?" "Mine. I have been interested in space since Year 5." "How often do you look at the stars?" "Every clear night. Last week I saw the rings of Saturn." I couldn't believe it. The boy who sat next to me for eight years was an astronomer and a bike mechanic, and I had never asked. Davyd's questions for me were good too. "Which book did you read twice this summer?" he asked, and I had to admit that it was a cookbook. At the end of the lesson Ms Olena collected the cards. "What did you learn today?" she asked. "Word order in questions," said somebody. "And one more thing," said Davyd. "We don't know each other as well as we think." This year I am going to ask more questions.
