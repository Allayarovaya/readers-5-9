Lesson 6

The Handkerchief Test

Listen & Read
Listen to the text
Speed:
How did people clean their carpets a hundred and thirty years ago? They carried them outside, hung them on a rope and beat them with a stick. The dust flew into the air and, unfortunately, into the noses of everybody around. In 1901 a young English engineer, Hubert Cecil Booth, went to see a new American machine for cleaning railway carriages. It blew air at the seats. Clouds of dust rose, hung in the air and slowly fell back onto the same seats. "Why don't you suck the dust in instead of blowing it away?" Booth asked the inventor. "Impossible," the man answered. "It has been tried." Booth couldn't stop thinking about it. A few days later he was sitting in a restaurant. He put his handkerchief on the soft seat of a chair, pressed his lips to it and sucked the air in as hard as he could. Then he turned the handkerchief over. There was a dark spot of dust on the other side. The idea worked. The other guests probably thought that he was mad. His first vacuum cleaner was not a thing you could keep in a cupboard. The prototype was as big as a cart, worked on petrol and was pulled by horses. It stood in the street in front of a house, and long tubes were passed through the windows into the rooms. The noise frightened horses, and neighbours complained. But the carpets were clean, and rich families began to invite friends to "vacuum parties", where guests drank tea and watched the dust travel through a glass tube. A few years later an American caretaker, James Spangler, who suffered from dust at work, made a light machine from a broom handle, a pillowcase and a small electric motor. It could be carried by one person. He sold his idea to a businessman called Hoover, and the machine came into ordinary homes. Today a round robot cleans the floor while its owner is at school. It finds its own way around the legs of chairs and goes back to its place to charge. It is a long way from a horse cart to a robot. And it started with a man who was not too proud to kiss a dusty chair in a restaurant.
