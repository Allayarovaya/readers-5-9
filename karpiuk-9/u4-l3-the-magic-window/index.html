Lesson 3

The Magic Window

Listen & Read
Listen to the text
Speed:
In the 1950s people called television "the magic window". For the first time in history a family could sit at home and see a football match, a concert or the surface of the Moon. Neighbours gathered in the one flat that had a TV set, and children sat on the floor with open mouths. Seventy years later the magic window is in every home, and often in every room. Is it still magic? Let's look at the statistics. Surveys in different countries show a similar picture. A typical adult spends about three hours a day in front of a screen with films, series and shows. Pensioners watch the most, sometimes five or six hours. Teenagers watch less traditional TV than their parents, but they spend even more time with video on phones and laptops. If you add it all up, the numbers are massive: by the age of seventy a person may spend nine or ten years of life just watching. Doctors use the expression "couch potato" for a person who sits on the sofa for hours with snacks and a remote control. It sounds funny, but the results are serious: extra weight, bad sleep and tired eyes. Psychologists add another problem. When watching becomes an obsession, real life starts to look slow and boring. Nobody in real life solves a crime in forty minutes. But it would be unfair to blame the window for what we do with it. Television has taught millions of people about nature, history and science. A good film can make us kinder. A news report can show what is happening on the other side of the planet. And watching a match or a quiz together is still one of the simplest ways for a family to spend an evening. The secret is in two words: choose and stop. A couch potato switches the TV on and watches whatever is on. A smart viewer opens the TV guide, chooses one or two programmes and switches the TV off when they are over. My grandfather, a pensioner, has his own rule. He watches the news, one film and all the matches of his favourite team. Then he goes to his garden. "The window is magic," he says, "only if you remember that there is also a door."
