Lesson 5

The Woman Who Wrote a Program Before Computers

Listen & Read
Listen to the text
Speed:
Every October people who work in science and technology celebrate a day that is named after a woman who lived two hundred years ago. Her name was Ada Lovelace, and she is often called the first computer programmer in history. Ada was born in London in 1815. Her father was Lord Byron, who was one of the most famous poets of his time. Ada never knew him: her parents separated when she was a baby. Her mother, who was afraid that the girl would become a dreamer like her father, decided to give her an unusual education. Ada studied Mathematics and science, which were not considered subjects for girls in those days. The girl loved machines. At the age of twelve she decided to build a flying machine. She studied the wings of birds, tried different materials and wrote a small book with drawings, which she called "Flyology". When Ada was seventeen, she met Charles Babbage, a mathematician who had designed a calculating machine. Babbage, who was forty years older than Ada, was surprised by the questions that she asked. They became friends and wrote letters to each other for many years. Babbage's next project was the Analytical Engine, which was planned as a huge mechanical machine working on steam. It was never finished. But in 1843 Ada translated an article about it from French and added her own notes, which were three times longer than the article itself. In these notes she described how the machine could be given instructions step by step. Today such a set of instructions is called a program. Ada understood something that even Babbage didn't see. He thought of a machine that counts. She wrote that a machine which works with numbers could also work with music, pictures and words, if they were turned into numbers. It is exactly what our computers do. Ada Lovelace died in 1852, when she was only thirty-six. Her notes were forgotten for a century. Then, in the age of the first real computers, engineers read them again and were amazed. A programming language that was created in the 1970s was named Ada in her honour. She once called her way of thinking "poetical science". Her father, whom she never met, would probably have understood.
